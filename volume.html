Volume II — Verification Suppression

The Rational Criminal, Revisited

Housing quality, chronic disease, and what institutions choose not to see.

Asia Lakay Grady Field Research 12 min read

Becker's rational criminal model assumes a calculating actor weighing cost against consequence. It says nothing about the institution that chooses not to look — the landlord who declines to document, the agency that closes a file early, the system that treats absence of record as absence of harm. This is where the model breaks, and where verification suppression begins.

The empirical case for this extension does not come from abstraction. It comes from inspection records, self-certification forms with missing signatures, and a maintenance pattern that drops off exactly when a legal filing becomes likely. The pattern is not incompetence. It is a rational response to the cost of being seen clearly.

When an institution can choose whether harm is documented, the absence of documentation stops being neutral. It becomes a decision.

The Housing Case

Chronic disease research treats housing quality as a background variable — present, but rarely causal in the models that determine funding or liability. Verification suppression argues the opposite: that the withholding of documentation is itself a mechanism of harm, distinct from and compounding the physical conditions it conceals.

Community-controlled weatherization reframes the remedy: not better compliance enforcement from the outside, but verification infrastructure controlled by the people who live the consequences of its absence.

Toward a Cooperative Remedy

This is where the framework connects outward — to cooperative economics, to Liberation Logic's nested control systems, to the broader thesis that resilience requires residents to hold the tools of verification themselves, not petition institutions to use them fairly.
